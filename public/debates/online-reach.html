FIELD · free-speech law, platform design & political psychology

Is reach speech, or is it design — and who said what

In 2022 Jonathan Haidt blamed a decade of American dysfunction on the machinery of virality more than on anything anyone posted, and argued that a democracy could rebuild the machinery without censoring a word. Can it? A dozen positions on that question fold into four disagreements, and the four fold into one question: whose hand is on the dial. Below are the history, a field guide to who holds which view, and the four cruxes, each argued by its strongest advocates on both sides.

One axis runs under all of it. Watch the two colors.

I  the lineage

The fight over the loudspeaker is older than the feed

Each node is a work, a ruling or a product change that moved the argument, and its halo shows which way it pushed. Verdigris treats distribution as part of the speech. Ochre treats it as machinery someone built and can rebuild. Lilac asks who gets to hold the switch.

1859
John Stuart Millphilosophy · On Liberty
Mill's worry ran past the law: “Protection, therefore, against the tyranny of the magistrate is not enough: there needs protection also against the tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling.” That sentence is why a platform's throttle can offend the value of free expression where the First Amendment never reaches. It cuts both ways: one pole aims it at the platforms, the other at the online mob.
1963
Bantam Books v. SullivanUS Supreme Court · distribution
Rhode Island's Commission to Encourage Morality in Youth banned nothing. It sent distributors lists of “objectionable” titles, mentioned its duty to recommend prosecutions, and the books stopped moving. The Court, 8–1: “We hold that the system of informal censorship disclosed by this record violates the Fourteenth Amendment.” The jawboning fights of the 2020s start here.
1969
Red Lion Broadcasting v. FCCUS Supreme Court · broadcast
Upholding the FCC's fairness doctrine, which made broadcasters air contrasting views, the Court wrote: “It is the right of the viewers and listeners, not the right of the broadcasters, which is paramount.” It's the high-water mark for a regulator shaping distribution for the audience's sake, and it rested on scarce airwaves. The FCC dropped the doctrine in 1987, and in Reno v. ACLU (1997) the Court found “no basis for qualifying the level of First Amendment scrutiny” online.
1995
McIntyre v. Ohio Elections CommissionUS Supreme Court · anonymity
Margaret McIntyre was fined $100 for handing out unsigned leaflets against a school tax levy. The Court sided with her: “Anonymity is a shield from the tyranny of the majority.” The next citation in the opinion is Mill.
1996
Section 230Congress · platform law
Twenty-six words: “No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider.” With a second clause protecting good-faith moderation, they put the gate in private hands, shielded from most suits over users' posts and free to choose what to carry.
2007
South Korea's real-name rulelaw · identity
From 2007 Korea's biggest sites had to verify users' real identities before they could post; by 2009 the rule covered any site with 100,000 daily users, and Google shut off uploads and comments on YouTube's Korean site rather than comply. A 2011 hack exposed data on more than 35 million Nate and Cyworld users. In 2012 the Constitutional Court struck the rule down unanimously.
2009
The retweet, then the shareengineering · virality
Chris Wetherell led the Twitter team that built the retweet button in 2009; eight days after the 2012 election, Facebook answered with a Share button on mobile. Haidt dates the break to features like these. Watching the first mob use his tool, Wetherell thought, as he told BuzzFeed News: “We might have just handed a 4-year-old a loaded weapon.”
2017–18
Tim Wu, Zeynep Tufekci, Renée DiRestalaw · sociology · disinformation research
The threat moved from the censor to the feed. Wu: “it is no longer speech itself that is scarce, but the attention of listeners.” Tufekci: “The most effective forms of censorship today involve meddling with trust and attention, not muzzling speech itself.” DiResta gave the design camp its motto, in Wired: “free speech does not mean free reach. There is no right to algorithmic amplification.”
2019
Mike Masnickessay · protocols
“Protocols, Not Platforms” proposed taking the dial away from the company so that “anyone could design their own interfaces, filters, and additional services.” Fukuyama, Richman and Goel called it middleware a year later; in 2024 Bluesky let users stack moderation services run by outsiders.
2022
Jonathan Haidtessay · social psychology
Why the Past 10 Years of American Life Have Been Uniquely Stupid (The Atlantic, April) blamed the 2010s on how things spread more than on what was said. Leaning on Frances Haugen's testimony, it called for architecture that slows virality, a bank-style “know your customer” duty with pseudonyms still allowed, regulatory oversight, data for researchers, and no social media for children until about 16. To a Senate panel that May: “Architectural changes, don't focus on content moderation.”
2022
Musk's Twitterplatform · policy
Three weeks after buying Twitter, Elon Musk posted its new rule: “New Twitter policy is freedom of speech, but not freedom of reach.” Weeks later, Bari Weiss's installment of the Twitter Files, “Twitter's Secret Blacklists,” presented the old management's “visibility filtering” as a scandal. Same tool, opposite verdicts, sorted by whose hand was on it.
2023
The election studies, and Parlerpolitical science · experiments
The US 2020 Facebook & Instagram Election Study turned the dial on tens of thousands of consenting users for three months: a chronological feed, no reshares, a third less like-minded content. What people saw changed a lot; no measured attitude moved, polarization included. Critics later argued that Meta's post-election emergency measures had altered the comparison feed. Months earlier, a PNAS Nexus study found that taking Parler offline hadn't reduced its users' activity on fringe sites overall. Each covers one platform at one moment.
2024
The Supreme Court on the gatelaw · Moody, Vullo, Murthy
In Moody v. NetChoice the Court called compiling and ordering others' posts “expressive activity of its own,” so a state can't order a feed rebalanced to suit its own idea of balance. In NRA v. Vullo, 9–0, it held that officials “cannot attempt to coerce private parties in order to punish or suppress views that the government disfavors.” Murthy v. Missouri, the big jawboning case, ended on standing with the merits unreached. The platform holds the dial; how hard the government may lean on its hand was left open.
2025
The FCC and Jimmy Kimmelregulator · pressure
FCC chairman Brendan Carr suggested on a podcast that broadcasters carrying Jimmy Kimmel risked “fines or license revocations,” adding: “we can do this the easy way or the hard way.” That day Nexstar, which needed FCC approval for a merger, pulled the show, and ABC suspended it. Kimmel was back within a week.
Into the present

In March 2026 Murthy ended in a consent decree barring three agencies, for ten years, from threatening platforms into removing or reducing the plaintiffs' posts, “including through altering their algorithms.” In June the Supreme Court let the president fire FTC commissioners at will. ABC is suing the FCC over an early review of its station licenses. Australia's under-16 rule has blocked nearly 5 million accounts and now faces its first major High Court challenge. Each side can point to a dial being turned by someone it distrusts.

II  the positions

A field guide: who holds what, and where it sits

Each card's edge is colored by pole. The tag is rough sociology, how widely the view is held today among the lawyers and researchers who work on this, inside the platforms and out. It isn't a verdict. The left column is about what governments may require or lean on; the right is about what platforms and their users do with the dial.

The state laws, regulators & pressure

Amplification law is speech law the US legal default
Daphne Keller · the Moody v. NetChoice majority · NetChoice
A rule that tells a platform what to rank down regulates what the platform says and what its users can reach. Calling it a design rule doesn't change what it does to speech.
Keller isn't absolutist: she writes that “some versions of amplification law would be flatly unconstitutional in the U.S.,” while a few rules “grounded in content-neutral goals, including privacy or competition” might survive.
Keller, Amplification and Its Discontents
No jawboning unanimous in principle
Bantam Books (1963) · NRA v. Vullo (2024) · Justice Alito
When officials lean on a platform to throttle a speaker, the speaker loses reach without any law to challenge. That's censorship by proxy, and it does the most damage when nobody announces it.
The principle won 9–0 in Vullo. Applying it split the Court in Murthy, which turned on standing, and that case ended in 2026 with a consent decree, not a ruling.
Regulate the plumbing EU law · US proposals
Jonathan Haidt · Frances Haugen · the EU Digital Services Act
Rules that touch no particular post — limits on reshare chains, a click before resharing, data access for researchers, a feed option that isn't built from your profile — change the incentives without anyone judging content.
The DSA also makes the largest platforms assess and reduce “systemic risks,” a far looser standard. Its first fine, €120 million against X in December 2025, was all plumbing: the blue checkmark, the ad archive, data for researchers. Weeks later the State Department barred five Europeans it accused of censorship, among them Thierry Breton, the former commissioner responsible for digital affairs.
Verify before you amplify spreading in legislatures
Haidt · Australia's under-16 law · the UK Online Safety Act
Before a platform amplifies an account to strangers, it should know that one real person of the right age stands behind it. The person can keep posting under a pseudonym.
Someone has to hold the proof. South Korea's stricter real-name rule ended in its Constitutional Court in 2012.
Anonymity is the shield strong in US case law
McIntyre v. Ohio (1995) · EFF · Jeff Kosseff
Speaking without a name has protected pamphleteers and people hiding from abusers. In the EFF's words, “Age verification systems are surveillance systems”: whoever holds the list holds the speaker.
Most anonymity cases are about the state unmasking a speaker. A platform choosing to verify is a different legal question, if not a different risk.
Protect the listener's attention minority in doctrine
Tim Wu · Zeynep Tufekci · Red Lion (1969)
When attention is the scarce thing, flooding and harassment silence more people than any censor, so a free-speech law that only watches the government misses the main threat.
Red Lion rested on scarce airwaves, and in Reno v. ACLU (1997) the Court declined to carry that reasoning online.

The platforms companies, crowds & users

Free speech, not free reach industry standard
Renée DiResta · Meta's “remove, reduce, inform” · X under Musk
Leave the post up and stop recommending it. Demotion is the humane middle course between deleting speech and pushing it into a million strangers' feeds.
Meta has been “reducing the spread of problematic content that does not violate our policies” since 2016. The slogan has united people who agree on little else.
Throttling is censorship you can't see loud, cross-partisan
Bari Weiss's Twitter Files · Human Rights Watch
A demotion is a removal with no notice, no stated reason and no appeal. The speaker can't see it, so they can't contest it, and the platform never has to defend it.
The complaint comes from both directions: Human Rights Watch reviewed 1,050 cases of suppressed content on Meta's apps in late 2023, and 1,049 were peaceful posts in support of Palestinians. Its cases were solicited, not sampled.
Deplatforming works strong where it's measured
McCabe et al. (2024) · Chandrasekharan et al. (2017) · Jhaver et al. (2021)
Remove the accounts that spread the most and the spread drops: fewer shares, fewer newcomers, less talk about the banned figures, and calmer followers left behind.
These studies measure the platform that acted. The Reddit team itself allowed that the ban may have driven users “to darker corners of the internet.”
Suppression backfires contested
Jacob Mchangama · Horta Ribeiro et al. (2023) · Andrew Callaghan
Bans and prosecutions move believers somewhere darker, where nobody argues back, and confirm the story that someone powerful is hiding the truth.
Callaghan's view is mixed: he backs some bans (Alex Jones, Andrew Tate) and credits social media itself with spreading QAnon. The Parler study measured where users went, not whether they grew more extreme.
Add context; let the crowd judge spreading
X's Community Notes · Meta · Ovadya & Thorburn on “bridging”
Don't remove or demote. Attach a note that raters who usually disagree both find helpful, and the gate moves from the company to a crowd that has to agree with itself.
Meta dropped its US fact-checkers for this model in January 2025, with Zuckerberg saying they had been “too politically biased.” Rewarding agreement across camps is a viewpoint of its own: it favors whatever the middle can accept.
the hinge ↕
Every feed has a gate. Fight over the hand on it.

Middleware, protocols, stackable moderation: Fukuyama and his co-authors want “to enable users to choose how information is presented to them,” Masnick wants filters anyone can build, and Bluesky lets users subscribe to moderation services the company doesn't run. Once you grant that no feed is neutral, whether a gate should exist stops being the question; the live one is who holds it and who can take it away. Put the reader's hand on it and the censorship objection mostly falls away, since a filter you chose silences nobody. What remains is the empirical worry the cruxes raise: readers may choose the angriest feed anyway.

Tags describe how the people working on this split today, not who's right. A view can be the legal default and still be wrong, and a minority view can be the one the evidence ends up favoring.
III  the cruxes

Four fair fights over the same dial

The positions are bundles of answers to these four questions. Crux 01 asks what reach is; 02 and 03 ask what the evidence shows, and the panels keep what a study measured apart from what each side reads into it; 04 asks who can be trusted with the dial. Each panel gives one side's best case in its own voice.

Crux 01 — the definitionlaw · philosophy

Is limiting reach a restriction on speech?

Four tools get called censorship, and they aren't the same: removing a post, banning an account, ranking a post down, and adding friction (a prompt, or a copy-and-paste step). This crux is about the last two. Nobody has a right to an audience, yet a speaker whose posts quietly stop showing up has lost something.

▸ distribution is part of the speech
Civil libertarians, the anti-jawboning courts, shadowban critics
A voice that can't travel isn't free in any way that matters, and a demotion is the most dangerous limit because nobody sees it happen.

The law worked this out sixty years ago. Rhode Island's morality commission banned no books in Bantam Books; it wrote to the distributor, “Cooperative action will eliminate the necessity of our recommending prosecution,” and the books vanished from the stands. The Court called that censorship. A post the feed won't carry sits in the same place: published on paper, unread in practice. Demotion is also the limit hardest to fight. A removal comes with a notice and an appeal; a demotion comes with nothing, which is why Human Rights Watch had to define “shadow banning” before it could document it. And friction isn't neutral in its effects. An extra step barely slows a senator with a press office, while it can sink an unknown with one good post and no other channel. Limiting reach limits listening too: when the 2020 election study took reshares out of Facebook feeds, users saw less political news, untrustworthy sources included, and ended up measurably less informed, while their political attitudes didn't move. The legal right and the wider value split here. A private platform may throttle whom it likes, but a public square run by a few companies that quietly turn people down is the “tyranny of the prevailing opinion” Mill warned about, with an algorithm doing the work.

▸ every feed is a ranking somebody chose
DiResta, Haugen, Haidt
There's no natural amount of reach to take away. Every feed is a product decision, and changing yesterday's decision silences no one.

Ask what “limiting reach” is measured against. A post's audience comes from a ranking model tuned to keep people scrolling; a chronological feed is a design too, and so is the retweet button. No baseline exists that the speaker owns. What the speaker owns is the right to speak and to be found by anyone who goes looking, and a demoted post keeps both. “There is no right to algorithmic amplification,” as DiResta put it. The law already treats volume this way: in Ward v. Rock Against Racism the Supreme Court let New York run the sound system at the Central Park bandshell, because a rule about decibels isn't a rule about lyrics. A cap on reshare chains is a rule about decibels. It applies to every post and doesn't care what the post says. “No one is censored by being forced to click on a link before resharing it,” Haugen told the Senate, and in written answers she added that capping reshare chains at two hops “has a similar impact on misinformation to the entire third party fact checking program”: the same result, with nobody deciding what's true. Haidt's case against the current design is itself a speech case. He asked senators to “please stop talking about content moderation” and to “focus on the fear of speaking up.”

the crux Is distribution part of what the speaker says, or a setting on the machine? The same demotion reads as either, depending on whether you picture the speaker or the feed.
Crux 02 — the evidence on banssocial science

Does suppression shrink extremism, or feed it?

What the studies measured: posts, shares, toxicity scores and site visits, on the platform that acted or on the sites users fled to, plus one cross-national count of far-right violence. What none of them measured: whether anyone's beliefs became more extreme, or less. Neither “censorship radicalizes” nor its opposite is settled.

▸ the ban moves the room, not the belief
Mchangama, migration researchers, Callaghan
A ban doesn't delete a belief. It moves the believers somewhere darker and hands them proof that someone is hiding something.

Watch what happens when the gate slams hardest. In January 2021 Google and Apple dropped Parler from their app stores and Amazon took it offline. Researchers following Parler users' browsing found that the shutdown “increased the probability of daily activity across other fringe social media” and did not reduce their activity on fringe sites overall; Gab and Rumble prospered. When r/The_Donald left Reddit for its own site it lost more than half its posts, but its remaining users showed rising signals of toxicity and radicalization. Counter-speech can reach a doubter on a mainstream feed. It can't reach them in a channel nobody else reads. Then there's the grievance. Andrew Callaghan, who has spent years filming America's fringes, says being deplatformed “only serves to validate their thesis, which is that they're the target.” A comparison of 18 West European countries found that the most far-right violence came where heavy public repression of radical-right actors and opinions met high immigration and weak radical-right parties, and its author warned that measures meant to contain the radical right “appear to fuel extreme right violence.” The studies that show success measure the platform that acted, and the cost lands where they didn't look.

▸ smaller rooms reach fewer people
Deplatforming researchers, trust-and-safety teams
Measured where the ban happens, the effect is large and points one way: remove the biggest spreaders and the spread falls.

After January 6 Twitter suspended some 70,000 accounts trafficking in misinformation; a study of more than 500,000 active users found that misinformation circulation fell, both from the banned accounts and among their followers, and that many traffickers who weren't banned left on their own. The authors can't separate the ban from January 6 itself, so the size is uncertain; the direction isn't. When Reddit banned its worst hate communities in 2015, the members who stayed cut their hate speech “by at least 80%,” and the subreddits they moved into didn't inherit it. After Twitter banned Alex Jones, Milo Yiannopoulos and Owen Benjamin, posts about them fell by about 90 percent. Migration is real, but it sorts: the committed follow and the curious don't, so the audience collapses. On Gab, banned Twitter users kept an average of 167 followers, down from about 7,700. A fringe site full of believers is a problem. A mainstream feed recommending the same posts to millions of strangers is a bigger one. The martyrdom effect is plausible, and it hasn't been shown at scale. That's thin ground for leaving the megaphone on.

the crux Do you judge a ban by how many people it stops reaching, or by what it does to the people it pushes out? The first gets measured. The second, so far, mostly gets argued.
Crux 03 — identityprivacy · psychology

Does anonymity mainly enable abuse, or protect dissent?

Three regimes that get blurred: full anonymity; verified pseudonyms, where the platform knows who you are and the public doesn't (Haidt's proposal); and real-name rules, where everyone knows (South Korea, 2007–2012).

▸ the name on file is the target
EFF, Kosseff, the McIntyre majority
The people who most need to post without a name have the most to lose, and any verification database is a list of their names waiting for a subpoena or a breach.

South Korea ran the experiment. From 2007 its biggest sites had to check users' real identities, and by 2009 the rule covered any site with 100,000 daily users. In 2011 hackers took the personal data of more than 35 million Nate and Cyworld users, national registration numbers included. The Constitutional Court struck the rule down unanimously in 2012, and noted that the regulator's own research found no evidence that defamatory or insulting posts had “diminished significantly.” The behavioral premise is shaky too. In a study of German petition firestorms, “non-anonymous individuals are more aggressive compared to anonymous individuals”: people attack in the open when they think they're enforcing a norm. After England players were racially abused during Euro 2020, Twitter said 99 percent of the accounts it permanently suspended were “identifiable,” meaning it held a verified phone number or email for them. The platform could trace almost every one of them, and the abuse came anyway. A verified pseudonym is still an entry on a list. Whoever holds the list — a company that gets subpoenaed, a government that changes hands — holds the whistleblower, the abuse survivor and the teenager who isn't ready to come out.

▸ accountability changes behavior, and bots aren't people
Haidt, researchers of online incivility
Nobody needs to know your name. The platform needs to know you're one real adult and not a thousand bots before it hands you a megaphone.

Haidt's proposal is not South Korea's. “Not that you have to post with your real name,” he told the Senate, “but just as you can't go to a bank and give them a bag of money, and say, ‘Open an account,’ banks have know your customer laws.” Verification would kill the cheapest tool of influence operations, the sockpuppet army, and let platforms enforce the age limits they already claim to have. Several comment-board studies point his way. In one study of newspaper comments, 53 percent of anonymous comments were uncivil, against 29 percent of comments under real names, and a study of Korea's own law found “significant effects on reducing uninhibited behaviors at the aggregate level,” even if individual users changed little. Twitter's “identifiable” meant an email address on file, which is not what Haidt means by verification. Korea's mistake was making every portal store national ID numbers; a third-party check that confirms a person and an age, and passes on nothing else, builds no such list. Haidt's measure of harm is the speaker who goes quiet. Verifying users, he said, “would make us less afraid to speak.”

the crux Is the bigger threat to a vulnerable speaker the anonymous crowd, or whoever holds the list? Verified pseudonymity is a bet that the list can be kept safe.
Crux 04 — powerinstitutions

Can any regulator stay viewpoint-neutral when power changes hands?

Haidt called for regulatory oversight of the platforms. The test is what that regulator does once the other party appoints it, and what counts as pressure when nobody writes a rule.

▸ build the dial for your friends, and your enemies will use it
Civil libertarians, Justice Alito, fairness-doctrine historians
Every power to shape distribution will eventually belong to someone who despises you, and “neutral” speech rules have a long record of partisans using them.

The fairness doctrine sounded like balance. Bill Ruder, a Commerce official in the Kennedy years whose PR firm ran the Democrats' 1964 radio campaign, later said: “Our massive strategy was to use the Fairness Doctrine to challenge and harass right-wing broadcasters and hope that the challenges would be so costly to them that they would be inhibited and decide it was too expensive to continue.” Fred Cook, the writer whose complaint became Red Lion, had been paid $1,500 by that campaign's front group. Sixty years on, the FCC had no rule about late-night monologues, only a chairman telling a podcaster “we can do this the easy way or the hard way,” and Jimmy Kimmel was off ABC within hours. In 2026 the FCC ordered ABC's stations to file for license renewal early, and ABC sued, calling it a “retaliatory campaign.” The government that settled Murthy by promising three agencies wouldn't threaten platforms is the one whose FCC chairman threatened broadcasters. And in June 2026 the Supreme Court held that the president may fire FTC commissioners at will, overruling what was left of Humphrey's Executor. The independent regulator reformers had in mind now serves at the pleasure of whoever wins. The more judgment a rule leaves (“harmful,” “misleading,” “systemic risk”), the more the regulator's politics fill it.

▸ the cure for partisan regulators is rules with nothing to aim
Haugen, Haidt, defenders of the DSA
Write rules that never ask anyone to judge content (reshare limits, data access, age floors) and a partisan regulator has nothing to point them at.

Look at what went wrong in each example: an official with discretion over content. The fairness doctrine made officials judge “balance,” and Carr invoked the FCC's rule against “news distortion,” another content standard. A reshare limit has no such dial. It applies the same way to every post from every side, which is why Haugen argued that content-free fixes “reduce the chance of bias on the part of Facebook on which content is actioned.” Courts can tell the difference. In October 2026 a federal appeals court held that Florida's age limit for social media accounts “likely passes First Amendment muster because it is a content-neutral speech regulation,” and the coercion rule in Vullo was unanimous. Researcher access reveals what platforms do and suppresses nothing, and a regulator can stick to plumbing: the EU's first fine under its new platform law went to X's blue checkmarks, its ad archive and its data for researchers, without judging a single post. The status quo isn't neutral either. The gate today belongs to a few executives who rewrite the rules when ownership or politics change. Meta dropped its US fact-checkers thirteen days before the 2025 inauguration; asked whether Zuckerberg was responding to his past threats, the incoming president said, “Probably.” A public rule can at least be read and challenged in court.

the crux Is the danger discretion, which careful drafting can remove, or power itself, which the next government inherits however careful the drafting? ↳ Crux 03's question, asked of a government instead of a database
IV  the payoff

Four cruxes, one question underneath

Crux 01 looks like a quarrel about definitions, but neither side denies that every feed is a gate. The reach-is-speech side objects to particular hands on it; the design side objects to treating today's engagement ranking as the speaker's property. Cruxes 02 and 03 keep turning on who did the gating: a platform banning accounts, app stores and a cloud host erasing a whole app, a portal storing ID numbers. Crux 04 asks it outright. Plot the positions by what they think reach is and by whose hand is on the dial, and the disagreement takes a shape: wide where the state holds the dial, narrow where the reader does.

Crux 01 · definition
What a demotion is

The horizontal axis. It looks like the whole fight and turns out to be the part both sides can draw: every feed ranks something.

Crux 02 · evidence
What a ban does

Measured on the platform that acted, argued everywhere else. The results change with who held the gate and how much of it they closed.

Crux 03 · identity
Who holds the list

The vertical axis at small scale. Verified pseudonyms are only as safe as the company, or the government, that keeps the names.

Crux 04 · power
Who holds the dial next

The vertical axis itself. Every rule is written by one government and enforced by the next.

Find your own hinge. Picture the dial in your own hand and most of this map agrees with you; picture it in your opponent's and the same dial looks like a gag. Work out whose hand you're imagining when you say reach is speech or reach is design, and you'll usually find the other person imagining a different one.
